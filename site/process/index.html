Summary

The diagnosis. The current page was written for a RevenueCat that took monetization off a PM's plate. Its promise was "You ship what matters, we handle monetization." The product has since outgrown that promise. Paywalls, Experiments with predicted 12-month LTV, web funnels and web billing now put monetization in the PM's hands. The page still describes the earlier product, and so do competitors' comparison pages: one lists four capabilities as missing from RevenueCat that RevenueCat now ships.

The positioning. Monetization is a product decision, and RevenueCat lets product managers make it like one. That principle organizes the page but never appears on it. The page is built on three jobs, drawn from the seven pain points PMs face: change it without a release, learn what users value, and fit the offer to every user, on a foundation that handles the rest. Revenue is the outcome, not the PM's job title.

The page. The hero reads "Build a subscription experience worth renewing." Eight section headlines tell the whole argument on their own. Five dynamic modules, including an experiment simulator where judging by conversion and judging by predicted 12-month LTV pick different winners.

How I worked. Claude as a strategy partner, Claude Code as the builder, five custom review agents, and a public repo where every decision is logged.

Framing and research

What I read first. RevenueCat's homepage, seven feature pages, the changelog and docs for what's actually shipped and when, the pricing page, and the State of Subscription Apps 2026 report for plausibility checks. Then five competitors' pages, plus the two alternatives PMs actually weigh: building in-house and native store tools.

What the scan showed. The category has converged on one promise to PMs: change the paywall without engineering, test it, read the analytics. That promise is now table stakes. RevenueCat's edge is not a unique feature. It is being the category leader with the most complete platform, a 12-month forecast inside the experiment, and scale no challenger matches.

The lesson: where a claim lives matters

Where the claim appearsWhat it meansDoes it block RevenueCat from claiming it?
Dedicated persona page or homepage headlineOwned. They own that message to that audienceYes. Don't lead with the same frame
Homepage section or feature pageClaimed. Strong but contestableOnly if RevenueCat can't prove it more completely
Docs, changelog, help centerParity. The capability exists, it isn't a positionNo
I challenged the AI

When the competitor agent found revenue-based winners buried in two competitors' docs, the AI concluded RevenueCat should retreat to the one claim nobody else could make: "grow revenue on the system that already runs it." I rejected it. A capability in someone's documentation is parity, not positioning, and a category leader shouldn't position like a challenger. We wrote the owned, claimed and parity rule into the agent so it wouldn't make the same mistake twice.

The AI challenged me

I wanted to add "autopilot," the system picking and shipping winners automatically. The AI pushed back on three counts: rollout is human-triggered in the product, Autopilot is a competitor's product name, and it contradicts the control idea. Its reframe was better: the machine recommends, the PM decides.

An agent caught it

All three PM personas rejected "monetization is a product decision" as customer-facing language: a truism, a turf claim, and wrong for enterprise. But each one, unprompted, described one of our three jobs in their own words. That validated the structure and killed the phrase as copy. Unowned is not the same as resonant.

An honest note. The final idea is close to where round one started. What changed is everything underneath it: it now comes from the PM's own job, it's structured by their real pains, and it's differentiated by proof rather than by a clever phrase.

The system: five agents and a context file

I built the page with a small review system rather than a chain of prompts, so every claim, headline and visual got checked by something that wasn't the thing that wrote it.

AgentIts jobWhat it caught
pm-criticReads everything as a solo PM, a growth PM and an enterprise PMValidated the three-job structure; surfaced the enterprise governance question and a list of product gaps. On the finished page: a clear win over the current one for the solo and growth PM, partial for enterprise
competitor-watchChecks every claim against what competitors own, claim or merely haveKilled a Superwall headline I planted as a calibration test, without being told it was one. Flagged that v0.1's core idea was Superwall's language
web-copyDrafts and tightens landing-page copy, always with a version cut by 30%. Used here for line-level cuts onlyEvery cut it proposed was applied before the build: 14 cuts, about 85 words (11%)
brand-guardCompares the built page with RevenueCat's visual grammarFive fixes in phase 3B, all applied: a leftover CSS rule that broke the funnel layout; section 3 tabs that were the largest blue fill on the page; section 7 icon tiles that read as the old feature grid; testimonials reworked into one centered block; and a container narrowed from 1,200px to about 990px, RevenueCat's width. Final verdict on the finished page: reads as a RevenueCat page, with no contrast or color-rule failures
claim-auditorTraces every claim to a public source56 fails in total, including a final sweep of 48 strings that failed 4. Among them: a product name that doesn't exist publicly, a testimonial with no speaker, "one click" where the product says "one action," Figma designs that come in through a separate plugin, a Benchmarks band drawn at 25th to 75th percentile where RevenueCat uses 20th to 80th, and "win-back," which RevenueCat uses for users who have already left

The context file. CLAUDE.md holds the audience, the non-negotiables, the design tokens, the contrast rules and the color roles. Every session and every agent reads it first.

The repo. Public, with the assignment brief, my notes, and the RevenueCat animations, product images and feature-page captures I studied kept out of it. Every decision is logged in docs/iteration-log.md.

Context states intent. Tooling enforces it. CLAUDE.md was created containing an em dash, breaking its own rule against them. So the rule became a pre-commit hook that blocks the commit. Then the hook was logged as built before it existed, a mistake the AI in chat made and caught itself on a later pass. Now it's real, and it blocks.

I challenged the AI

What the agents missed: a page for product managers that never once said "product manager" in its visible copy. Four agents had reviewed it. None was asked to check who the page speaks to. I caught it on a read-through. Agents check what they're told to check.

Architecture and copy

Architecture decisions

  • The hero carries the idea; the sections carry the proof.
  • Half static, half dynamic. The current page is entirely static imagery, and visual storytelling is the first thing being evaluated.
  • One fictional app across every visual, Tidelark, a premium AI morning-routine coach, so the page tells one story instead of four unrelated ones.
  • Zigzag layout for the job sections, like RevenueCat's own feature pages.
  • "Learn more" links to the matching RevenueCat feature pages, so a PM can go deeper.

The hero: 19 lines considered

CandidateVerdict
"Your paywall shouldn't wait for a release."The strongest felt pain, but table stakes, and close to Superwall's promise. Became section 4's headline
"Change the paywall today. See its 12-month value."Two ideas with no link between them, and "change" is an activity, not a capability
"Test pricing like you test features."No outcome, no benefit, no difference
"Ship the paywall that pays, not the one that converts."A competitor claims revenue over conversion, and "pays" reads as a guarantee
"Own your paywall without waiting on engineering."Superwall's positioning, planted on purpose to test whether competitor-watch would catch it. It did
"Launch a paywall today. Judge it on the next 12 months.""Today" isn't true for a first paywall, and the first half sits on Superwall's promise
"Stop shipping paywalls that win the week and lose the year."The sharpest idea, but it opens by telling the reader they're wrong. Became section 5: "Win the year, not just the week."
"Build the subscription experience your users stay for."The right idea. "Paywalls" narrowed it, "subscription experience" widened it
"Build a subscription experience worth renewing."Kept. A subscription is renewal by definition, so "worth renewing" is the user-value idea in two words. Top combined score across the three PM personas
"Build the reason they renew."The cheekiest line. Lands best once the argument is made. Became the closing line, and the hero test variant
I challenged the AI

The first three hero lines the AI offered were weak, and I said so. They satisfied the rules, short, accurate and unowned, but didn't lead with what the reader gets. The wordplay that won, a subscription being renewal by definition, was mine.

The hero animation

The first version had a cursor clicking three cards around a phone. The clicks changed nothing. The rebuild follows the hero's own sub, in about eight seconds: Design (the featured plan changes on the phone), Test (weekly against monthly, with B pulling ahead), Roll out (a bar fills to 100%, then "No app release.").

I challenged the AI

A cursor clicking cards that change nothing is decoration, not a demo. I also pushed for a paywall that looks like a real, premium app rather than two plan rows. Every click in the new loop visibly changes what the user sees.

Section 3, three versions

Three arrow rows linking to the sections below, which was a table of contents pretending to be a section. Then tabs with a before-and-after drag slider. Then side-by-side panels, each built on its own idea. Change shows who owns each step: before, Engineering and the App Store own most of the track; with RevenueCat, every step says "You," and it reaches "Live" first. Learn shows paying customers as dots: week one favors A, the predicted year favors B. Fit contrasts one paywall for everyone, in the app only, with the right offer for each audience, in the app and on the web. The slider went because it only earns its place when what it reveals is strong enough.

One detail I like: I asked for B's dots to stay lit. The builder kept them fading too, just far less than A's, because that's what the model says. Honest proportions beat a cleaner picture.

The skimmer test

A PM who reads only the headlines should get the whole argument.

  • 1Build a subscription experience worth renewing.
  • 2Trusted by teams at [logos]
  • 3Change faster. Learn what renews. Fit every user.
  • 4Your paywall shouldn't wait for a release.
  • 5Win the year, not just the week.The test ends. The learning doesn't.
  • 6Different users. Different offers. App or web.
  • 7Subscribe once. Unlocked everywhere.
  • 8Two ways to start.
  • Closing:Build the reason they renew.

The simulator, four versions

VersionWhat it didWhat was wrongWho caught it
v1A toggle swapped one metric and the winner flippedThe tension only existed across two moments in time. The rollout message appeared on the user's phone, where users would never see itThe AI's review
v2Both metrics on every card; the PM's panel separated from the user's phone; a real paywall; a one-time cursor demoIt showed what won, not why. The numbers were too small to feel real, and the decision too obviousI did

The AI challenged me

My idea for v3 was a crossover: variant A leads for three months, then B overtakes. With annual versus monthly plans that's impossible, because annual plans are paid upfront, so B would lead from day one. Someone who knows the subscription data would spot it immediately. We switched the test to weekly versus monthly, one of the most debated pricing questions in subscription apps, and the story became true.

An agent caught it

Even then, illustrative numbers still have to be plausible. The audit tested the model against RevenueCat's own published conversion and retention data and refit every input to those ranges, keeping the story: A leads early, B overtakes at month 4 and ends about 47% higher.

The AI challenged me

I wanted the winner to roll out automatically once the forecast appeared, so visitors wouldn't have to click. Automatic rollout is autopilot, which the product doesn't do and which we had rejected on purpose. Instead, the cursor demo performs the rollout itself, so every visitor sees it happen, and the PM still decides.

Two gaps I pushed to close in the final pass. The page showed charts as tables, so the Charts module now uses real line charts under RevenueCat's own chart names. And web billing, one of the most important new capabilities, was barely mentioned. It now has its own lines in section 6, in RevenueCat's own public phrasing, with no fee percentages.

Brand: rebuilt, not reused

I studied seven RevenueCat feature pages and two of their product animations, then rebuilt the visual grammar in code rather than reusing their files. The work had to be my own, and the reviewers would recognize their own assets. In production, I'd pull components from the design library and brief the agency for anything new.

  • Colors sampled pixel by pixel from RevenueCat's pages: off-white #F9F9FB, a lavender and peach hero wash
  • Color roles: blue for actions, green for winning and live states, red for emphasis
  • RevenueCat's fonts are commercially licensed, so the page uses Manrope for display and RevenueCat's own declared fallback stack for body text
An agent caught it

Three accessibility failures in the published brand palette: white text on brand red, brand green on white, and brand blue as link text on the off-white. Red moved off buttons, matching the live site, which already uses blue. Green became a fill instead of text. Links moved to a darker blue that passes everywhere.

Verified: 390px first, then 1440px, in a headless browser whenever the preview wasn't visible. Keyboard operation, screen reader announcements for state changes, focus handling, reduced motion, and every outbound link returning a valid page. Bugs caught and fixed include a style rule that showed both paywall states at once, compound words breaking at their hyphens on mobile, and a toast that covered the status line.

The red team: the new page against the current one

Before the last pass, I stepped back and tested the page against the brief and against the current /for-product page, side by side, the way a reviewer would.

The PM's roleBystander: RevenueCat saves engineering timeOperator: change it, learn from it, fit it

ProofOne CTO quote about engineering timePixelcut, Floga, OpenAI, and RevenueCat's scale: 146K+ apps, $17B+ processed

What it found, and what changed:

  • Section 7 broke the story. "Decide who can change a live paywall" was a governance feature that repeated a section 4 bullet, next to a visual about something else. It became the foundation: "Subscribe once. Unlocked everywhere."
  • Two truths from the current page had gone missing: Customer Center, and Apple and Google API changes handled for you. For a page promising "worth renewing," losing the retention feature was a real gap. Both came back in section 7.
  • One pain had no home. Blind spots between acquisition and revenue now map to campaign tracking in web funnels.
  • Density. The web-to-app journey went from seven steps to four, at half the height, and tabs stopped scrolling sideways on mobile.

Then all three agents reviewed the finished page. pm-critic called it a clear win over the current page for the solo and growth PM, and a partial one for the enterprise PM, which led to a security and compliance link on the Enterprise card. brand-guard found no contrast or color-rule failures. claim-auditor swept 48 strings, failed 4, and all 4 were fixed.

An agent caught it

Pixelcut's quote sat right beside the simulator, celebrating a 16% increase in subscribers, and its case study credits a weekly plan. So the proof point was praising exactly the kind of early win the simulator argues can mislead, and anyone at RevenueCat who knows that case study would have seen it. The quote moved to section 4, where it proves fast iteration instead of undercutting the forecast.

Questions I'd bring to Product

These came from pressure-testing the page with synthetic PM personas. They're hypotheses to validate with real customers, not findings.

  • An approval step before a change goes live
  • Reverting an offering or an experiment
  • A holdout group after a winner rolls out
  • A confidence range on the 12-month forecast, since credible intervals currently cover conversion metrics only
  • Comparing past forecasts with what cohorts actually earned
  • How long "enough data" takes for a low-traffic app
  • Proactive alerts, since revenue anomaly emails are in beta

Small fixes I'd queue for the web team

The audit surfaced a few inconsistencies across RevenueCat's own pages. The kind of thing a PMM who owns the site fixes in week one:

  • The AI Editor is labeled beta in the docs and generally available in the changelog
  • The product demo linked from the Paywalls page predates the Figma import and the AI Editor, so it can't show either
  • A Funnels page sentence reads "full control of price points on promotions," which looks like a typo
  • The current /for-product page's demo dashboard link now lands on a login screen

With more time: a guided product demo

The simulator is a small version of what I'd build next: a guided demo a PM can play with before signing up. Pick your app category, build a paywall from a template, run a simulated test against benchmarks for similar apps, and roll out the winner. Five minutes, no account, no SDK. The current page's demo dashboard link now lands on a login screen; this would replace it with a guided version, aimed at PMs. It could take voice input, but voice isn't the point. The point is reaching the aha moment before the signup form.

One rule: every answer it gives comes from RevenueCat's own data and docs, never free-form AI. A demo that improvises will eventually promise something the product doesn't do.

How I'd measure it: demo completion, then signup rate from the demo, then first paywall published within a week.

A positioning idea for later

RevenueCat's scale lets it answer a question no challenger can: not "did B beat A," but "is my number any good?" Benchmarks shipped in April 2026. Bringing that context into every decision a PM makes is the strongest position I found that the product doesn't yet fully support.

Assumptions

  1. The page serves prospects and existing customers, and the mix is unknown. It leads for prospects and adds one link for customers.
  2. The primary conversion is self-serve sign-up. Sales is secondary.
  3. PMs own the product experience and strategy. Who owns the revenue number varies by company size.
  4. Only public, shipped capabilities are claimed. Nothing from any roadmap.
  5. All product data is illustrative and labeled. Tidelark is fictional.
  6. RevenueCat's fonts are licensed, so the page substitutes.

What I couldn't verify

Real traffic, real customer interviews and internal data. The PM personas are synthetic, and their feedback is treated as hypotheses.
